Why ice floats — and how it melts back into water

Same H₂O molecules, two ways of packing them. Frozen, they lock into an open honeycomb lattice that takes up ~9% more room — so the solid is lighter than the liquid, and it floats.

ρice ÷ ρwater = 0.9167 ÷ 0.9998 = 0.9168 — and exactly that fraction of a floating cube, 91.7%, sits below the line, leaving 8.3% in the air. Flipped around: freezing expands water by +9.1% in volume, which is why pipes and bottles burst.

Why it floats

Water pushes up on anything submerged with the weight of the water displaced (Archimedes' principle). A floating object sinks only until it has displaced its own weight — ice gets there at 91.7% submerged. Push the cube deeper and the extra displaced water shoves it straight back up.

Why ice is the odd one out

Almost every substance is denser as a solid and sinks in its own liquid. Water flips this: each H₂O forms up to 4 hydrogen bonds, and below 0 °C they lock into the open hexagonal lattice you see above. The enforced holes mean ~9% less mass in every cm³.

Melting without warming

While it melts, incoming heat is spent breaking hydrogen bonds, not raising temperature — the cube holds at exactly 0 °C until the last crystal is gone. That toll, the latent heat of fusion, is 334 J per gram: enough to heat the same gram of water from 0 to 80 °C.

The level never moves

A floating cube already displaces exactly the volume its meltwater will fill, so the surface doesn't rise by a hair as it melts — watch the dashed line. Same reason melting sea ice barely changes sea level, while melting land ice raises it.
